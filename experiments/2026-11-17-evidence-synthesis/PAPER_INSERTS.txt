Evidence-grounded additions for the EDM-IR manuscript
2026-11-17. Draft prose; not yet merged into LaTeX.

Scope and measurement units

We distinguish changes in execution semantics from changes in the latency
model. Our recent experiments use four balanced FP16 All-to-All--GEMM
development shapes on eight A100 SXM4 80 GB GPUs connected through NVLink.
The shapes are (P,M,K,N)=(8,64,8192,1024), (8,512,8192,512),
(8,64,4096,2048), and (8,256,8192,512), where M denotes rows per
source--destination block. These synthetic shapes do not establish the
frequency of equivalent operators in a routed MoE workload.

The three recent collection windows contain 42,000 raw timing samples.
This count describes repeated executions, not independent shape coverage.
The November 11 and November 16 windows check correctness on three input
seeds but measure latency on one seed. The final window measures three
seeds, each under six balanced candidate orders. We summarize each
candidate within a process block by the median of 100 executions, and
report both cross-block summaries and block-level selection losses.
Profiling runs are separate from the unprofiled latency labels.

Numerical admission and candidate identity

Latency comparisons are restricted to candidates passing all prescribed
full-output checks. Failed candidates remain in the record and are not
assigned artificial high-latency labels. Candidate identity includes the
implementation, reduction precision, execution context, and correctness
contract. In particular, FP32 accumulation inside a GEMM does not imply
that intermediate split-K outputs avoid FP16 rounding. The serial FP16
feedback implementation exhibits failures that can be missed by sampled
output checks. Sixteen newly measured FP32-partial implementation--shape
combinations pass all 48 prescribed checks, although this is not a proof
for arbitrary inputs.

The original geometric feature vector maps six admitted FP16/FP32
reduction pairs to identical inputs despite measured FP32 slowdowns of
6.64--27.99%. This is a concrete representational collision. Accounting
for partial-buffer precision and final conversion distinguishes these
candidates; reusing the FP16 latency as an FP32 label would conceal the
cost of satisfying the numerical contract.

Baseline and execution evidence

Host-side execution is not a single baseline. In the November 11 window,
the faster measured merged host implementation is 1.78--2.32 times faster
than the per-source host implementation across the four shapes. Relative
to the faster measured merged host, the best admitted fused candidate
achieves ratios of 1.74--3.08 in that window and 1.69--3.16 in the
November 16 window. These are measured candidate-oracle ratios within
each window. They do not quantify selector quality or serving speedup,
and the host implementations do not exhaust all possible library or
collective configurations.

Grouping has a conditional effect. For (8,64,8192,1024), doubling the N
group size increases latency by approximately 19--20% at q=1 but reduces
latency by 7.3--9.8% at q=8 in the November 11 measurements. This sign
change rules out logical request reduction alone as a sufficient latency
criterion. The transformation also changes CTA count, warps per CTA,
staging requirements, and split-K execution depth; these effects are not
isolated by that comparison.

A matched staged-pipeline comparison is available on two development
shapes. Relative to its serialized control, the pipeline reduces latency
by 10.33--16.36% on M512 and 7.09--9.99% on large N across three blocks.
It remains slower than the measured merged host and direct fused paths.
This establishes a benefit for the tested schedule, not an optimal
pipeline or a measured fraction of hidden communication.

Scoring model and development ablation

The frozen W1 scorer is a shared log-latency ridge regression, not an
iteratively trained neural classifier. It minimizes weighted squared
log-latency error with a fixed ridge coefficient of 0.01. Shapes receive
equal mass, with equal host and fused mass within a shape. Host/fused
selection is induced by the lowest candidate score after admission.

In leave-one-development-shape-out diagnosis, extending the original
geometry with coupling and reduction-precision features reduces mean
selection regret from 12.60% to 1.22% and maximum regret from 47.36% to
3.05%. Pair-direction errors decrease from 74/393 to 63/393. However,
both variants still select a non-optimal candidate on two of four shapes.
On these same observed shapes, a retrospective fixed q4/g2 comparator
has mean regret of 1.04% and maximum regret of 2.92%. Consequently, this
ablation supports mitigation of severe errors, not superiority over
simple selection rules or independent generalization.

Final balanced-order replication

For (8,64,4096,2048), we freeze both scorers before the final collection
and measure six candidates under three seeds and six balanced orders.
The aggregate medians are 623.575, 626.784, 631.104, and 645.252 microseconds
for q2, q4, q4/g2, and q8/g2, respectively; the merged cuBLASLt and
CUTLASS hosts take 1170.600 and 1176.205 microseconds.

Both frozen scorers select q8/g2. This candidate is fastest in none of
the 18 blocks, with aggregate regret of 3.48%, mean block regret of
4.68%, and worst-block regret of 8.42%. All six aggregate pairwise
orders among the four fused candidates are reversed by the scorer,
whereas all 144 block-level fused-versus-host comparisons favor fused.
Thus a correct coarse execution-family decision can coexist with an
incorrect within-family ranking. The 144 comparisons come from one
shape and do not establish bidirectional host/fused accuracy.

The distinction between decision loss and predicted benefit is material.
The coupled scorer predicts q8/g2 to take only 61.74% of q2 latency;
the measured aggregate ratio is 103.48%. The resulting modest regret
therefore does not imply a calibrated relative performance estimate.
q2 outperforms q8/g2 in 17 of 18 blocks, while the closely spaced q2,
q4, and q4/g2 candidates exchange ranks. Removing an entire seed group
can change the aggregate winner from q2 to q4. We retain every block
and avoid claiming a universal ordering among these close candidates.

Diagnosis and limitations

Exhausting all 720 permutations of the six-candidate input list leaves
each frozen model's scores and decision unchanged. Auditing the eight
saved ridge solutions gives relative normal-equation residuals below
6.14e-17. These checks do not support enumeration order or incomplete
optimization of the specified ridge objective as explanations for the
observed errors.

The large-N fold instead extrapolates on two shape axes: its training
shapes have K=8192 and N in {512,1024}, while the query has K=4096 and
N=2048. Geometric counts and their interactions are mapped to latency
by unconstrained regression coefficients; they are not measured
nonnegative service times. Rank deficiency alone also does not explain
the two examined fused score contrasts, whose training-nullspace
projections are negligible. The evidence points to limitations in the
learned execution-cost relation and its transfer across shape
combinations, without uniquely attributing the error to a particular
loss, regularization term, or hardware contention mechanism.

The final window uses a new session identity. It is a descriptive
replication of frozen predictions, not silent admission of the old
model into a new execution contract. The six reserved shapes remain
unevaluated. These experiments do not demonstrate BF16 behavior,
real expert-routing frequencies, end-to-end MoE or vLLM speedup, or
an intrakernel communication-overlap fraction. The predictor is
retained with these limitations rather than further tuned on the
replication outcomes.
